# China and the European Digital Commons EDIC

## Public Digital Commons, Technological Sovereignty and Prospects for Strategic Complementarity

**Research memorandum — 29 October 2026**  
**Intended use:** independent strategic assessment and source corpus for a later, detailed comparison against the adopted **Digital Commons EDIC Implementation Strategy 2026–2027**.  
**Status:** analytical working paper. It does **not** describe an agreed policy, partnership, funding commitment, or position of the Digital Commons EDIC (DC-EDIC) or any Chinese institution.

### Method, evidence and terminology

- The assessment prioritises primary sources: Chinese central/municipal government notices; the organisations' own governance documents; the European Commission; the DC-EDIC; the Eclipse Foundation; the Open Source Initiative (OSI); and project-maintained activity metrics. Government-hosted reporting of announced programmes is identified as such. Each substantive factual claim has an inline reference of the form **[S##]**, resolved to a full title and URL in the **Source register** at the end.
- **Established fact** means directly supported by cited text. **Programme commitment** means a published target, eligibility criterion or announced funding envelope, *not* proof of realised spending, adoption or impact. **Analytical inference** is explicitly labelled and should be tested. **Proposal** denotes an option developed in this memorandum, not an existing negotiation.
- Chinese labels are retained to make later Chinese-language searches possible. Terms such as *open source*, *open model*, *public digital infrastructure* and *digital commons* are not presumed equivalent. Governance, source availability, licensing, contribution rights and operational independence must be assessed project by project.
- The geographic sample is **not exhaustive**. The selected regions have unusually clear, actionable policy texts or established programmes. Absence of a locality from the sample is not evidence that it lacks initiatives.
- The DC-EDIC's full adopted **50-page Implementation Strategy** is publicly available and should be treated as the **authoritative European baseline**, rather than a press summary: [Official PDF](https://digital-commons-edic.eu/news/2026-10-27-assembly-decisions/implementation-strategy-2026-2027.pdf). [S01]

## Executive assessment

**Principal finding.** Mainland China has no identified institution with the *same legal form, member-state governance and public-administration deployment mandate* as the European DC-EDIC. It nevertheless possesses a substantial **functionally analogous ecosystem**: a centrally encouraged open-source industrial strategy; the **OpenAtom Foundation** and project stewardship infrastructure; regional innovation and open-source financing programmes; volunteer-governed communities such as **KAIYUANSHE**; academic/standards actors; and real cross-border cooperation with European open-source foundations. [S01–S07, S10–S24]

**Strategic distinction.** European policy frames digital commons as a means to create verifiable, interoperable, supplier-replaceable and sustainably governed digital capabilities for public administrations and the broader ecosystem. Chinese public policies place comparatively stronger emphasis on domestic capability, strategic software and hardware stacks, supply-chain resilience, industrial adoption, innovation and commercialisation, while also explicitly encouraging international open-source participation. This is an **interpretation of the cited priorities**, not a claim that all Chinese projects are state-controlled or that all European projects have fully independent governance. [S01, S02, S04–S08, S12–S19]

**Most consequential existing precedent.** The **Eclipse Foundation–OpenAtom Foundation** partnership is not hypothetical. It includes a cross-foundation arrangement involving **OpenHarmony and Eclipse Oniro**, a coordinated Chinese translation of the Eclipse Public License 2.0, and a renewed exchange in March 2026. Eclipse's 2026 project metrics show continuing work on Oniro. The partners themselves describe the arrangement as a **“sister projects” model** with distinct domestic/international project structures and technical coordination. This is unusually strong evidence that scoped, open-governance Europe–China cooperation can be implemented without requiring a single jointly controlled institution. It **does not** independently establish that the arrangement is suitable for DC-EDIC or security-critical public deployments. [S25–S30]

**Best initial opportunities, ranked:**

1. **Comparative digital-commons governance and sustainability evidence** — particularly policy, licensing, contribution mechanisms, project health, financing and anti-capture safeguards. **High thematic fit; relatively low operational exposure.**
2. **Critical open-source dependency intelligence, maintenance and software-supply-chain assurance** — evidence exchange and joint work on globally upstreamed non-sensitive components, subject to controls. **High thematic fit; moderate operational complexity.**
3. **Open-source programme offices (OSPOs), public procurement and reuse patterns** — compare public-sector approaches without treating Chinese industrial procurement subsidies as equivalent to Europe's shared-workspace deployment commitments. **High learning value; limited direct deployability.**
4. **Open AI governance, provenance and licensing** — particularly the distinction between *open weights* and systems meeting the OSI Open Source AI Definition. **Medium–high learning value; substantial terminology and compliance risks.**
5. **Public-interest, non-personal environmental data and open interfaces** — a possible narrowly scoped technical pilot, especially where EU–China research channels already operate. **Medium fit; potentially manageable political risk.**

**Recommended institutional sequencing:** start with a public, non-binding **evidence exchange** between open-source foundations/communities; document results and governance safeguards; only then consider a DC-EDIC-authorised initiative or cross-border code collaboration. Do not propose a common strategic government IT platform or shared handling of sensitive data as an initial step. [S01, S02, S32–S37]

## 1. The DC-EDIC baseline: what must be matched

The DC-EDIC is a legally constituted **European Digital Infrastructure Consortium**, established by Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2025/2170 under the Digital Decade framework. It pools Member State efforts around open digital infrastructure and digital commons; its policy objective is to strengthen European capacity and reduce technological dependency. [S03, S02]

The **adopted** Implementation Strategy, dated **16 October 2026**, is more specific than a general notion of technological sovereignty. It proposes an operating logic of **Connect → Deploy → Maintain**, but work may begin at any of the three functions and evidence is expected to guide investment decisions. The strategy stresses subsidiarity, openness and neutrality, reliability, usability, European capacity, multiplier effects, evidence and proportionate operation. [S01, pp. 3–9]

### 1.1 The eight first-cycle results

The following list is transcribed in meaning from **Section 6.1**, pp. 23–24 of the official 50-page Strategy, which distinguishes seven operating products/services and a cross-portfolio roadmap. It is a starting index for the next research phase; it is **not** a completed implementation-level assessment. [S01]

| ID | DC-EDIC first-cycle result | Function | Evidence to investigate in China |
|---|---|---|---|
| E1 | Community Network and Local Nodes | Connect | National/municipal open-source community nodes; KAIYUANSHE local communities; OpenAtom regional programmes [S06, S07, S14] |
| E2 | One-stop shop and expertise hub | Connect | Legal/compliance services, incubators, public code infrastructure, OSPO support in Beijing, Shanghai and Zhejiang [S12, S13, S15] |
| E3 | State of Digital Commons | Connect | KAIYUANSHE's annual China Open Source Report; public programme reporting; X-lab research [S08, S09] |
| E4 | Deployment Challenge Programme | Deploy | OpenAtom competitions; incubation and deployment demonstrations; programme-level analogy only, not evidence of the same challenge design [S06, S17] |
| E5 | Collaborative Workspace Flagship | Deploy | **No sufficiently evidenced direct Chinese equivalent established** in this assessment; research government-reusable collaboration suites, operating entities and deployability rather than infer from operating-system programmes [S01] |
| E6 | Critical Dependency Intelligence | Maintain | Beijing and Shanghai SBOM/supply-chain provisions; open-source security and project metrics [S12, S13] |
| E7 | EU Sovereign Tech Fund Pilot | Maintain | Jiangsu fund architecture, Hangzhou project grants, OpenAtom stewardship and funding: **partial analogy, not equivalence** [S16, S18, S06] |
| E8 | Future Domains Roadmap | Cross-portfolio | Provincial AI/open-hardware plans; possible comparative portfolio-prioritisation methods [S12–S19] |

The Strategy identifies the **Collaborative Workspace** as the first proposed deployment flagship (initially document collaboration and messaging, with meetings subject to confirmation) and a maintenance-financing pilot operated by the **Sovereign Tech Agency** under EDCOM. The next-stage comparison must preserve these concrete objects, programme governance and the Strategy's six portfolio-review criteria, rather than conflating them with broad Chinese ecosystem metrics. [S01, pp. 3–4, 23–25, 46–47; S02]

### 1.2 Implication for international engagement

**Analytical inference.** Cross-border research, standards exchange and evidence-gathering may complement the Strategy's **Connect** and **Maintain** activities; they do not automatically satisfy **Deploy**, which requires proven user journeys and repeatable adoption across European public organisations. Any new programme outside existing EDCOM work needs the required authority, resources and governance decision. [S01, Sections 4, 6, 8]

## 2. China's central strategic architecture

### 2.1 From the 14th Five-Year Plan to the 2026 AI + Software policy

China's **14th Five-Year Plan and 2035 objectives** explicitly supported digital-technology open-source communities, more effective open-source intellectual-property frameworks, and enterprise openness in code, hardware designs and applications. The MIIT's software-industry development plan called for domestic open-source foundations, code-forge infrastructure, project/community incubation, governance and international cooperation. [S04, S05]

In **September 2026**, MIIT presented the **AI + Software Special Action Implementation Plan**, positioning open source as infrastructure for software and AI innovation. Its objectives include incubating **at least five major open-source projects by 2028**; strengthening code/model/dataset hosting; governance and licensing; developer incentives; and industrial adoption. MIIT officials identified **OpenAtom and AtomGit** as key platforms. At the press conference, OpenAtom reported **over 80,000 hosted models**, **370,000 datasets** and **over 12 million registered users** on AtomGit by August 2026. These are **officially stated platform statistics**, not independently audited outcomes. [S06]

**Interpretation:** the centre's logic is capacity-building and industrial resilience using open collaboration. It is relevant to DC-EDIC's maintenance, community and infrastructure objectives, but **a national hosting platform is not inherently a collectively governed digital commons**. Governance rules, transparency and access conditions must be checked independently.

### 2.2 OpenAtom Foundation — 开放原子开源基金会

OpenAtom, established in **Beijing in June 2020**, is a non-profit foundation supporting open-source project stewardship, incubation, governance, community infrastructure and industry participation. It is the strongest **functional analogy** to a public-interest open-source stewardship institution, although it is **not** a Chinese EDIC and its legal/governance construction differs from a Member State consortium. [S07, S06]

Prominent OpenAtom-related programmes and technical assets include:

| Asset | Relevance | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| **OpenHarmony** | Open-source distributed operating-system components for devices, embedded/IoT and sectoral uses | Do not equate OpenHarmony with Huawei's commercial HarmonyOS products; inspect actual project licence, upstream and project controls [S07, S25, S26] |
| **openEuler** | Open-source Linux ecosystem for enterprise/server infrastructure | Technical community governance is documented; independence must be assessed empirically [S10] |
| **openKylin** | Desktop/server operating system and multi-architecture adaptations | Explicitly identified as an OpenAtom-incubated project [S11] |
| **AtomGit** | Domestic collaborative source code and AI-model/data infrastructure | Hosting scale is a claim about use; verify openness, mirroring, exportability and cross-border reach [S06, S07] |
| **OpenLET, OpenLoong and newer sectoral communities** | Data, robotics and industry-specific common foundations | Potentially interesting for methods and interoperability; higher dual-use/data-sensitivity complexity [S06, S07] |

**Potential complementarity:** endowment/stewardship models, neutrality criteria, project incubation, source-licence compliance, open governance, release engineering, shared security processes, and reproducibility. **Limitation:** the present research has not established that any particular OpenAtom implementation should be procured for an EU government or included in the DC-EDIC's first-cycle portfolio.

### 2.3 Academia, CCF and open hardware

The **China Computer Federation (CCF)** and its open-source committees organise professional and academic work on project evaluation, contributor incentives, governance and education. Such actors offer an evidence and standards channel rather than the same operational mandate as DC-EDIC. [S22]

The **Chinese Academy of Sciences** has presented the **XiangShan** RISC-V open processor project and associated **Ruyi** software ecosystem; these initiatives demonstrate China's use of internationally open instruction-set architecture within a national strategic-capacity programme. They may be relevant to a later **Future Domains Roadmap** discussion, not necessarily to DC-EDIC's current workspace and maintenance portfolio. [S23, S01]

### 2.4 The licensing boundary matters

The **Mulan Permissive Software License v2 (Mulan PSL 2.0)** received OSI approval in **February 2020**. This demonstrates that Chinese-origin licences can be assessed and recognised through international open-source governance. However, approval of one Mulan licence does **not** automatically extend to every other licence in the family, nor to open-weight model distributions. The **OSI Open Source AI Definition 1.0** separately establishes requirements for AI systems. [S24, S35]

## 3. Regional public policies and their strategic instruments

**Important:** China's **Beijing, Shanghai, Chongqing and Tianjin are province-level municipalities**; Hangzhou and Shenzhen are cities within Zhejiang and Guangdong, respectively. This report groups policies by their real issuing level and does not mislabel a municipal decision as a provincial regulation.

### 3.1 Beijing: a multi-instrument open-source ecosystem plan, 2026–2028

The **Beijing Municipal Bureau of Economy and Information Technology's Open Source Ecosystem Construction Implementation Plan (2026–2028)** aims to develop **10 internationally influential projects** and **30 prominent domestic projects** and promote **100 industry-model use cases**. It supports foundational and industrial software; AI and RISC-V infrastructure; community incubation; code and compliance services; procurement/adoption cases; OSPO-style organisational capabilities; software bills of materials (**SBOM**); talent evaluation; and links with international open-source standards and institutions. [S12]

**Distinctive policy instruments:**

- Support for state-owned enterprises and public-interest bodies contributing code and participating in foundational communities.
- Support for government–enterprise development of open-source applications, *subject to security and compliance conditions*.
- Encouragement of SBOM in publicly funded software procurement, plus public safety/compliance services.
- Support for international organisations establishing a local ecosystem presence.

**Complementarity hypothesis:** Beijing is the strongest policy comparator for **Connect**, **Critical Dependency Intelligence**, public-sector procurement and the governance of open-source supply chains. Actual adoption, issued procurements, eligible suppliers and implemented SBOM coverage remain to be documented. [S12, S01]

### 3.2 Shanghai: public open-source systems policy and international governance language

The **Shanghai Municipal Government's Implementation Plan for Strengthening the Open-Source System**, issued December 2025, seeks by **2027** to incubate **200+ quality projects**, cultivate **100 open-source commercial firms**, develop **one or two internationally influential communities**, and attract **over three million open-source developers**. It covers AI tooling, RISC-V toolchains, community support, skills, overseas participation, licensing, supply-chain risk and SBOM standards. These are policy targets, **not achieved results**. [S13]

Of particular strategic interest, the September **2026 Open Source Innovation Development Conference**, supported by the **Shanghai Open Source Information Technology Association**, introduced an **Open Source Innovation International Cooperation Declaration**. The association's account explicitly describes **数字公地核心原则** (*core principles of digital commons*), international governance and coordinated resilience mechanisms. A Shanghai open-source investment-fund initiative was unveiled at the event. **Caution:** a conference declaration or a fund launch is **not** a national binding treaty, a government budget appropriation or proof of a mature financing vehicle. The full declaration text, signatories, fund terms and implementation status must be obtained before making stronger claims. [S20, S21]

**Complementarity hypothesis:** Shanghai offers an unusually explicit conceptual overlap with DC-EDIC on digital commons, governance, legal mechanisms, sustainability and international cooperation. A comparative study of **definitions, ownership and non-capture protections** would be more defensible than immediate joint deployment.

### 3.3 Zhejiang Province and Hangzhou: open-source contributions as a policy object

Zhejiang's 2026 provincial implementation framework targets, by **2027**, **1–2 globally influential communities**, **50+ quality open-source projects**, **150+ open-source enterprises** and **100+ deployment exemplars**. It promotes open-source organisational infrastructure, software/hardware/data, international engagement, contribution-based evaluation, and potential use of compute/model/data vouchers. The provincial government's own portal reports the seven-department policy and targets; the text has also been published as a reproduction of the notice. [S14]

Hangzhou's **2026 software-industry policy** provides grants of **up to RMB 1 million** for qualifying incubation-stage projects hosted by a national open-source organisation and **up to RMB 500,000** for each selected excellent project/product, within programme limits and eligibility criteria. [S15]

**Complementarity hypothesis:** contributor recognition, programme design, ecosystem entrepreneurship and incentives for release/upstream participation. **Important distinction:** project-incubation subsidies are not equivalent to a maintenance contract protecting a globally used library.

### 3.4 Jiangsu Province and Suzhou: an OpenAtom-linked regional financing ecosystem

Jiangsu's **OpenAtom Zijin** regional zone in Suzhou is described in provincial-government-hosted reporting as a pioneering province-level open-source innovation facility. In August 2026, the programme announced its first incubated projects and an investor/fund ecosystem, including an **OPC fund matrix planned at not less than RMB 1 billion** and an additional **RMB 30 million** AI micro-seed initiative. These amounts are **announced targets or programme envelopes**; the research has **not** verified paid-in capital, beneficiary-level disbursements, effective governance or investment returns. [S16]

**Complementarity hypothesis:** examine the separation between grants, venture financing, donations and maintainer support; identify which mechanisms could *complement* the European Sovereign Tech Fund rather than displace its public-interest selection criteria. [S01, S16]

### 3.5 Guangdong Province and Shenzhen: demand-side incentives

Guangdong announced open-source ecosystem support in the AI/robotics context, including a programme for up to **five selected communities or centres per year**, with a subsidy covering **30% of audited previous-year operating expenses**, capped at **RMB 8 million per project**, subject to policy eligibility. [S17]

Shenzhen's **2026 industrial equipment renewal and technology-upgrade measures** explicitly incentivise **OpenHarmony and RISC-V** adoption: eligible industrial upgrades involving OpenHarmony and meeting the minimum investment threshold may receive **up to 15%** support; qualifying RISC-V-equipped machinery may receive an **additional 5%**, with a **RMB 10 million per-project cap** under the stated programme. These are conditional subsidy rates, not universal payments. [S18]

**Complementarity hypothesis:** useful evidence on **demand creation** and industrial uptake of open-source foundations. **Constraint:** the industrial subsidy model does not directly answer whether a multi-country EU public administration can operate interoperable document/messaging suites while retaining provider choice. [S01]

### 3.6 Chongqing: Chance Foundation and OpenHarmony ecosystem programmes

The **Chongqing Tiangong Kaiwu Open Source Foundation (重庆天工开物开源基金会)**, also known in English as **Chance Foundation**, received registration approval from Chongqing authorities in **March 2023**. Its public-interest remit covers open-source software, hardware, open content, training, professional support and exchanges. [S19, S31]

Chongqing's 2026 **OpenHarmony application-innovation** plan describes a **1 alliance + 3 platforms + N scenarios** architecture and targets **50,000 developers by 2030**, concentrating on sectoral applications such as instrumentation, industrial control and transport. [S19]

**Complementarity hypothesis:** project maintenance and education models, non-profit foundation governance, and potentially community-level bilateral exchange; independent of any assumption of endorsement by the Chongqing municipality.

## 4. KAIYUANSHE and its institutional connections

**KAIYUANSHE (开源社; sometimes translated China Open Source Alliance)** was established in **2014** as a volunteer-driven, vendor-neutral and non-profit open-source community organisation with missions spanning governance, international bridges, community development and project incubation. It joined the **Open Source Initiative** as an affiliate in **2016** and publishes the **China Open Source Annual Report**. KAIYUANSHE is **not identical to OpenAtom** and should not be presented as a government agency. [S08, S09]

### 4.1 Connections evidenced by the organisation

- **COSCon**, the China Open Source Conference, is KAIYUANSHE's flagship conference. **COSCon 2026** is scheduled for **14–15 November 2026 in Hangzhou**, jointly organised with **Datawhale**. OpenAtom's **openKylin** community is presenting an event track, demonstrating an event-level interface between KAIYUANSHE and an OpenAtom-incubated project. An event relationship should not be misrepresented as a common board or formal joint venture. [S08, S11, S32]
- KAIYUANSHE identifies community cooperation with conferences and ecosystems involving **OpenAtom**, **CCF**, **COPU**, **CommunityOverCode/Apache**, **FOSSASIA**, **OpenInfra**, **OSPO Summit** and other international open-source communities. These are **network links**, not proof that KAIYUANSHE controls or represents the other organisations. [S08]
- KAIYUANSHE's OSI affiliation and role in cross-language licensing discussions represent independently verifiable international governance activity. [S08, S24]
- **Zhuang Biaowei (庄表伟)** has been publicly identified as the 2026 chair of KAIYUANSHE's project committee and an executive deputy secretary-general of **Chance Foundation**, providing a documented institutional overlap; this does **not** in itself authorise him to bind either institution. [S33, S22]

### 4.2 Current governance caution

According to its official **2026 Governing Board** page, KAIYUANSHE's chair is **Lin Lüqiang (林旅强)** and vice chair **Liang Yao (梁尧)**, alongside **Li Mingkang, Chen Yang and Xu Yin**. Older international speaker biographies naming **Nadia Jiang (江波)** as chair reflect her **earlier** tenure and should not be used to infer the organisation's current office-holders. [S09]

### 4.3 Strategic role

**Assessment:** KAIYUANSHE is potentially the best **community-governance and evidence partner** for studying the Chinese landscape because it combines international affiliation, domestic community links, publications and a vendor-neutral self-description. This should be corroborated by reviewing its voting rules, membership criteria, project selection process, publication methods and decision transparency. It is **not** the right substitute for an official government-to-government interlocutor.

**Public channels:** [KAIYUANSHE website](https://kaiyuanshe.cn/); [2026 board](https://kaiyuanshe.cn/department/board); [2025 China Open Source Report](https://kaiyuanshe.github.io/2025-China-Open-Source-Report/en/); [COSCon information](https://www.kaiyuanshe.cn/blogs/18). [S08, S09, S32]

## 5. Verified European precedent: Eclipse Foundation–OpenAtom

This is **the strongest verified cross-border implementation case in the entire map**.

1. **2021:** The Eclipse Foundation and OpenAtom announced their intention to cooperate around OpenHarmony, aiming for a global, vendor-neutral community. [S25]
2. **2024:** Eclipse announced a concrete collaboration agreement supporting **Oniro** as an international open-source project drawing on OpenHarmony. [S26]
3. **December 2024:** OpenAtom and Eclipse jointly announced an agreed Chinese translation of **EPL 2.0**. [S27]
4. **March 2026:** An OpenAtom statement described a visit by Eclipse's **Michael Plagge**, a discussion with **Li Bo**, and a continuing communication mechanism. It characterises the Oniro/OpenHarmony arrangement as **sister projects** in different institutional environments with coordinated technical development. [S28]
5. **2026 technical activity:** Eclipse project reporting and a mid-year project update document active contributions and practical implementation activity. The precise number of commits changes over time; use the **live metrics**, date-stamped, rather than a frozen headline number. [S29, S30]

**Strategic reading:** This case provides a practical benchmark for cross-jurisdictional source custody, governance rights, licensing, upstream/downstream contribution and independence. The model does not prove that equivalent arrangements are low-risk for sensitive public-sector systems, and it does not confer a mandate on DC-EDIC to participate. It should first be analysed as a *case study*.

**Questions for case-study due diligence:**

- Which entity owns trademarks, repositories and release authority for the relevant components?
- How are contribution rights, copyright checks, licence compliance and security disclosure distributed?
- Can each ecosystem sustain a build, security patch and fork without exclusive dependence on the other?
- What proportion of code flows upstream and downstream, and how are conflicting roadmaps resolved?
- Were there modifications to governance after the original agreement, and what does the 2026 working-group activity show?
- Which aspects are generalisable to reusable digital commons, and which depend on this particular embedded-OS ecosystem?

### 6.1 A crucial distinction about technological sovereignty

The DC-EDIC's sovereignty concept is best operationalised as **the demonstrable ability to understand, adapt, deploy, support, interoperate and replace digital capabilities**. China's central/regional plans often emphasise **domestic control of foundational technology and industrial ecosystem capacity**. These aims overlap through open development, supply-chain resilience and maintenance, but may diverge on public governance, data rights, supplier neutrality and reciprocal market access. The comparator should be **evidence of independence**, not geopolitical labels. [S01, S02, S04–S08, S12–S19]

## 7. Recommended cooperation concepts

### Concept A — International Digital Commons Governance & Sustainability Observatory

**Priority:** 1 / strongest recommendation.  
**Type:** comparative research and open methodology; no sensitive systems or personal data.  
**Possible participants, subject to invitations and authorisation:** European foundations, KAIYUANSHE, OpenAtom, Chance Foundation, academia and independent governance researchers.

**Outputs:**

1. A bilingual glossary distinguishing *digital commons*, *digital public goods*, *open source*, *shared government infrastructure*, *open-weight model*, *sovereignty* and *autonomy*.
2. A replicable framework for project governance: licensing, intellectual property, contributor rights, transparent decision-making, reproducible builds, fork rights, funding diversity, upstream relationships and succession/continuity.
3. A 15–20-project evidence inventory, with explicit evidence/unknown labels and no endorsement of individual products.
4. A public comparative report with actionable lessons for the DC-EDIC **State of Digital Commons** and **Critical Dependency Intelligence** results.

**Acceptance conditions:** source-linked data for every project; at least two independent cross-checks for critical claims; published evaluation rubric; right to publish critical findings; transparent handling of disagreements; evidence of European benefit.  
**Strategic rationale:** converts an ambiguous diplomatic issue into a falsifiable institutional study. [S01, S08, S12, S13, S28]

### Concept B — Critical Commons Maintenance and Supply-Chain Assurance Exchange

**Priority:** 2.  
**Type:** maintenance and security process research, with potential later open-source contributions.  
**Starting scope:** a small number of established, non-sensitive, globally upstreamed components that have clear licences, maintainers, vulnerability procedures and no restricted transfer of information.

**Outputs:** comparison of SBOM practices, dependency-criticality methods, vulnerability-handling workflows, maintainer funding models, package provenance and upstream contribution patterns; publication of non-sensitive findings and tools. [S01, S12, S13, S16]

**Constraints:** no exchange of undisclosed vulnerabilities without an approved secure procedure; no privileged access to government infrastructure; no transfers of personal data; export-control and sanctions screening before technical exchanges; independent security assessment of any deployed component. [S37–S39]

### Concept C — Public Sector OSPO, Procurement and Open-Source Reuse Practice

**Priority:** 3.  
**Type:** institutional learning rather than joint procurement.  
**Outputs:** comparative inventory of public buyers' selection criteria, contributor policies, contract clauses, ability to commission upstream maintenance, and evidence of actual reuse. [S01, S12, S14]

**Key test:** determine whether a public body has actually released and governed reusable code, or merely funded a local vendor's open-source-based implementation. These are materially different outcomes.

### Concept D — Open AI Commons and Licensing Taxonomy

**Priority:** 4.  
**Type:** standards/legal and technical evaluation.  
**Outputs:** cross-jurisdictional definition concordance, provenance and training-data information requirements, licence compatibility assessment, reproducibility cases and independent model-evaluation criteria. [S06, S24, S35]

**Key warning:** do not certify a model as *open-source AI* merely because weights, inference code or a model card can be downloaded. [S35]

### Concept E — Environmental Digital Public Goods Pilot

**Priority:** 5, contingent.  
**Type:** standards and reference implementation for **non-personal**, non-sensitive environmental observations, with independent EU and Chinese hosting.  
**Rationale:** the European Commission's existing China research agenda identifies climate/biodiversity and food/agriculture as areas of selective cooperation. This **does not** automatically establish financing eligibility or programme authority for a DC-EDIC project. [S36, S01]

## 8. Political, legal and operational constraints

1. **Mandate and governance.** The DC-EDIC's Assembly has strategic decision-making authority; its Director represents the body operationally; the Advisory Board provides advice. Exploration does not automatically authorise a partnership, budget or official representation. [S01–S03]
2. **European strategic autonomy.** Any collaboration should be demonstrably consistent with *supplier replaceability, independent maintainability, transparency and protection against capture*. [S01, S02]
3. **Third-country cooperation.** The EU maintains a selective and reciprocal approach to research and innovation with China. Participation and funding under Horizon Europe have explicit restrictions, and Chinese organisations are not automatically entitled to EU research funding. This applies to relevant research channels and should not be misrepresented as a blanket prohibition on open-source dialogue. [S36]
4. **GDPR and cross-border data.** Transfers of personal data outside the EU require a lawful transfer basis and protections. Design the initial pilots to avoid personal data entirely; obtain separate legal assessment if later proposals introduce it. [S38]
5. **Export controls and security.** The EU dual-use regime can cover software and technical assistance in relevant circumstances. Open source is not an unconditional exemption from every export or security restriction; assess actual functionality, recipients, end use and applicable exclusions with competent experts. [S39]
6. **Open-source quality versus label.** Require demonstrable licences and permission to study, modify, fork, redistribute and contribute, together with build/test evidence. For AI apply the proper definition. [S24, S35]
7. **Funding integrity.** Distinguish announced fund sizes from committed capital, grants from loans, venture investment from public-interest maintenance, and pilot selection from measured impact. [S15–S18]
8. **Political presentation.** The most defensible framing is **cooperation on governance, resilience and sustainability of globally shared open-source components**, not the formation of a bilateral technology-sovereignty bloc. This is a recommendation derived from the distinct mandates, not a quoted EU or Chinese government position. [S01, S02, S36]

## Final conclusion

China's policies and institutions show a genuine strategic convergence with Europe around **open technological foundations, supply-chain resilience, contribution ecosystems and sustainable software infrastructure**. The more difficult question is not whether China has an open-source strategy—it demonstrably does—but **which Chinese implementation practices can reinforce, rather than compromise, the DC-EDIC's independently governed European capabilities**.

The strongest evidence-led starting point is an **international comparative governance and maintenance study**, followed only if warranted by a **bounded, non-sensitive technical or policy pilot**. The existing Eclipse–OpenAtom relationship supplies a concrete cross-border case to examine. The full DC-EDIC Implementation Strategy supplies the necessary authoritative measurement framework. The next phase must validate actual Chinese delivery against the Strategy's **eight expected results and documented decision criteria** before proposing any formal institutional collaboration. [S01, S25–S30]
