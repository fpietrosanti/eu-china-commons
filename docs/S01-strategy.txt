Executive Summary

Europe has strong open-source communities, public-sector initiatives, companies,
maintainers and technical resources. Wider adoption remains difficult: needs,
expertise and deployment experience are scattered across countries and
organisations. Teams repeatedly solve similar integration and procurement problems,
while demand, support and maintenance priorities remain hard to coordinate.

The Digital Commons European Digital Infrastructure Consortium (DC-EDIC) will
organise European cooperation to connect these actors and needs, deploy digital
commons and maintain them at scale. It will preserve national choice, open
contribution and fair participation across the ecosystem. The DC-EDIC maintains the
shared agenda and comparable evidence; the best-placed actors deliver, once their
proposed roles and resources are confirmed.

Three priorities will guide the 2026-2027 cycle:

 � Connect: establish light European coordination to bring needs, expertise,
     resources and contributions together around shared problems.

 � Deploy: implement complete user journeys to make adoption repeatable and
     develop reusable components, interfaces and methods, preserving solution
     diversity and choice.

 � Maintain: assess critical dependencies and strengthen their resilience through
     long-term care and targeted funding.

Work can start with any function. Shared needs can prompt deployment; use can
reveal maintenance needs; dependency findings can change a deployment choice.
Evidence can redirect priorities at any stage.

The first cycle has eight expected results: seven operating products or services and
a Future Domains Roadmap for assessing later options and deciding which to
pursue. Each identifies its users or decision-makers, the result expected by
end-2027 and the evidence needed to evaluate it.

EDCOM provides the first funded programme: community activities, a one-stop
shop, strategic dependency assessments, the EU Sovereign Tech Fund Pilot (EU-
STF Pilot) and 100-day challenges. The Strategy brings them together around
common results, within EDCOM's contractual governance.

The Collaborative Workspace is the first proposed deployment flagship, bringing
contributors together to improve everyday tools, trusted interoperability and shared
components that sustain a diverse European market. Document collaboration and
messaging provide the initial use cases; meetings remain to be confirmed. Work will
combine component improvements with integration, adoption and long-term care.

The EU-STF Pilot, operated by the Sovereign Tech Agency within EDCOM, will test
European financing for selected critical open-source dependencies. Working with
trusted maintainers, it will assess public benefit and support efficiency, with
proportionate reporting and accountability. Maintain also covers dependency
assessment, security and long-term continuity beyond the pilot.

The first cycle will produce early results and use experience to improve them,
concentrating limited resources where work demonstrates value. Success will be
judged by:

 � coordinated action and European capacity: assessed needs and contributions
     lead to action, shared findings inform decisions, and countries and ecosystem
     actors reuse results;

 � adoption and deployment: organisations use open-source solutions in routine
     work, and shared improvements make deployment repeatable elsewhere;

 � maintenance and resilience: supported critical dependencies improve in
     remediation, continuity, maintainer capacity or adoption confidence.

By end-2027, the Assembly will review the portfolio and decide what follows,
including whether the DC-EDIC's own contribution justifies the coordination effort.
Each intervention should provide reusable methods, findings, relationships or
delivery capacity. Together, these results should strengthen Europe's capacity to
develop, deploy and sustain digital commons.

1. Mandate And Strategic Objective

1.1 The Mandate

Article 24 of the DC-EDIC Statutes requires an Implementation Strategy covering
actions and timelines; roles and responsibilities; resources; governance and
stakeholder contributions; implementation arrangements; milestones, targets and
deliverables; key performance indicators; and risks.
Article 3 and Annex V define the wider tasks: build a European community around
digital commons, facilitate access to funding and support, support maintenance and
scaling, contribute to strategic developments and organise collaboration on concrete
projects.
This Strategy sets first-cycle priorities within that mandate. It identifies which needs
come first, how the tasks reinforce one another and where limited European
resources and expertise can have the greatest effect.

1.2 The Policy And Delivery Context

The Statutes and Annex V establish the Strategy's legal and political mandate.
EDCOM provides its initial delivery base; the European Commission's Open Source
Strategy sets the wider policy context.
EDCOM funds community building, the one-stop shop, strategic dependency
mapping, preparatory EU-STF work and 100-day challenges. The DC-EDIC will build
on them within the programme's existing governance and contractual framework.
The Commission's Strategy links open source to sovereignty, resilience, public-
sector capacity and competitiveness. Within its mandate and capacity, the DC-EDIC
contributes by deploying secure alternatives with Member States, sustaining
strategic European assets, and jointly identifying and developing reusable open
digital assets. The Commission's supply- and demand-side measures inform the
priorities for first-cycle tests. Results from those tests will inform decisions on which
European capabilities to sustain or expand beyond 2027.
Rapid technological change requires adaptable instruments. Artificial intelligence (AI)
is changing software production, testing, documentation, security and support, while
cybersecurity obligations are strengthening. Cloud, identity, mobile-device and
collaboration architectures are evolving; procurement and licensing practices face
renewed scrutiny. The Strategy maintains a stable direction while adapting its
instruments to experience and findings.

1.3 The Central Objective

The objective for 2026-2027 is:

   Build the European capacity to connect actors and needs, deploy digital
   commons and maintain them at scale.

Dependable open-source adoption requires resources now spread across many
actors: technical assets, procurement and integration expertise, deployment
environments, support capacity and dependency evidence. Coordinating them

gather enough demand to sustain dependable integration, support and maintenance.

Shared work can make a practical difference. An infrastructure component can
reduce duplicated integration across countries; a tested procurement method can
reduce uncertainty for buyers and suppliers. Dependency evidence can direct
maintenance support, and a reusable deployment pattern can give a new participant
a tested starting point.

Member States gain from reuse, faster access to tested methods, stronger
negotiating power through credible alternatives and continuity for capabilities
supporting public services. Common requirements can make European demand
clearer to software companies. These effects strengthen European agency: the
ability to understand, adapt, deploy, support and replace critical capabilities.

The DC-EDIC will strengthen the actors already doing this work. Member States
retain policy and procurement choice; communities and maintainers retain technical
agency. Companies, integrators, hosters and support providers remain essential to
delivery. The DC-EDIC sustains the common agenda, makes evidence comparable,
coordinates responsibilities and preserves continuity across projects and countries.

1.4 A Focused First Cycle

The first cycle runs from Assembly validation to end-2027: enough time to deliver
services and pilots, within a credible horizon for a fast-changing field. It establishes
the operating model through tangible results using available resources and delivery
arrangements.

The end-2027 findings will shape the next work programme: which capabilities to
continue, scale or extend into other statutory tasks. The Future Domains Roadmap
keeps later needs available for assessment and decision.

Each intervention starts from a shared need or dependency, uses the smallest useful
response and supports a decision on what follows. The best-placed actor delivers it.
The DC-EDIC relies on partners' existing expertise, relationships and funded
responsibilities, acting directly where shared accountability, evidence or governance
needs a responsible European body. A proposed contribution is available only after
the actor confirms its role and resources.

Connect, Deploy and Maintain have equal strategic standing. Their budgets,
maturity, timetables and direct DC-EDIC workload reflect the smallest useful proof,
available funded instruments, confirmed partner capacity and evidence needed for
the next decision. Focus prevents effort dispersing across an activity catalogue;
shared results make pilots useful beyond their original setting.

2. Strategic Logic And Principles

2.1 The Operating-Capacity Gap

Adoption at scale requires open-source code and the capacity to integrate, use
and sustain it. Organisations need interoperability, security, usability, support and
maintenance suited to their context. They also need credible migration, appropriate
procurement terms, integration expertise and continuity when a component or
supplier changes.

The knowledge and means are distributed. A community may need procurement
expertise; an administration, integration support; an integrator, a common reference
profile. A funder may support maintenance when evidence identifies dependencies
blocking adoption.

Repeated national effort and deployment gaps weaken common demand.
Integrators, support providers and maintainers become harder to sustain, reducing
adoption confidence and reinforcing dependency on established proprietary offers.

The DC-EDIC links needs, delivery and dependencies around real cases. Component
ownership stays distributed; the DC-EDIC coordinates interfaces and responsibilities
and makes evidence usable.

2.2 Three Strategic Priorities

Connect organises light European coordination to turn distributed needs, expertise
and contributions into joint action. It qualifies requests, connects peers and experts,
maps actors and capabilities, and gathers adoption knowledge and findings for
decisions.

Deploy uses end-to-end use cases to build repeatable adoption paths and reusable
components, interfaces and adoption resources, preserving solution diversity and
choice. Defined user journeys focus each flagship; targeted challenges address
blockers exposed by real deployment.

Maintain combines dependency intelligence, stewardship and targeted financing to
strengthen the resilience of critical digital commons over time. It draws on
deployment evidence, maintainer knowledge and public-interest priorities to pilot
proportionate support, continuity and financing responses.

Each function has its own instruments and proofs. A dependency assessment can
change a deployment choice; a maintenance signal can prompt a coalition or use
case. Every substantial intervention will state what must be connected, what will be
deployed and what must be maintained.

2.3 Shared Conditions For Adoption

The DC-EDIC will concentrate on conditions where shared European action can
materially improve adoption: user journeys and acceptance criteria; interoperability
profiles, open standards and conformance tests; procurement, migration and
adoption methods; maturity, support and maintenance criteria; dependency and
public-value evidence; and review decisions.

Common requirements allow convergence while preserving choice. Different
components and national configurations can meet them. A reference implementation
may demonstrate that requirements work together; subsequent selection continues
to follow shared evidence and criteria.

Sharing can take different forms at each technical layer. Common standards and
tests support compatible implementations but require continuing specification and
conformance work. A common codebase allows shared development and
corrections while creating common dependencies and maintenance choices. Open

interfaces keep components replaceable. Partners choose the form that best serves
users, balancing autonomy, integration, coordination and lifecycle costs.

Reusing a component means adopting existing software. Pooling effort means
sharing resources or responsibilities for development, maintenance, testing or
operation. Joint support can strengthen the community already sustaining a widely
reused component.

2.4 Principles For Implementation

All Strategy activities follow these principles.

Subsidiarity. Address shared needs, create reusable value or resolve coordination
problems more efficiently at European level than through separate national or project
action. Responsibility for delivery stays with the best-placed teams and
communities. Strengthen suitable existing capabilities and support initiatives that
explore better approaches.

Openness and neutrality. Select and assess through transparent criteria, open
standards and fair contribution routes. Guard against hidden product endorsement or
capture by a supplier, country or community.

Reliability. Support adoption claims with evidence on security, support,
maintenance, continuity and operating responsibility.

Usability. Treat user experience, accessibility and organisational adoption as
technical quality. Demonstrate that people can use and operate the solution
effectively.

European capacity. Strengthen European communities, maintainers, product
companies, integrators, hosters and support providers so they can deliver and
sustain digital commons around real demand.

Multiplier effect. Focus on shared assets and decisions that mobilise partners'
expertise and resources and improve conditions for wider investment and adoption.
Generic foundations serving similar, stable needs across products are strong
candidates for shared development and maintenance. Product diversity can improve
workflows, user experience and capabilities.

Evidence and iteration. Use pilots to deliver and learn. Test credible hypotheses of
user benefit through bounded experiments. Base continued investment and wider
deployment on observed results and credible maintenance. Each pilot defines its
decision, evidence and conditions for stopping, adjusting or scaling.

Proportionate operation. Match technical convergence and shared responsibility to
the intended result. Use governance and reporting to protect public value and
support timely delivery, treating administrative burden as an implementation risk.

2.5 Collective Action

The DC-EDIC combines collective action with distributed delivery. A core group of
Member State partners and communities shares its objectives. A small coordinating
function maintains their common agenda and evidence, preserves continuity and
keeps activities focused on shared outcomes. It clarifies decision rights and
supports regular communication around decisions. Partners retain room to act and
clear accountability.

3. Connect: From Distributed Capacity To
Coordinated Action

3.1 Purpose

Connect will help European actors find the expertise and support needed to act.
Knowledge is spread across public administrations, communities, foundations,
universities, companies, integrators, hosters, Open Source Programme Offices
(OSPOs), funders and European programmes. Needs and contribution offers often
fail to reach an actor able to respond.

During the first cycle, Connect will route qualified European needs, barriers and
contribution offers to expertise, procurement support, deployment actions,
maintainers, funding instruments or governance decisions.

It serves both demand and supply. Public and private organisations need help
adopting and operating digital commons. Communities, maintainers and European
suppliers need clearer demand signals, contribution routes, and access to
deployment environments and expertise. Connect brings them together around
concrete cases.

The first cycle will test whether this light structure advances real cases and makes
findings from different sources useful for common decisions. Success means
progress from a qualified need to a useful next step. Directory size, contacts, events
and reports are supporting activity measures.

3.2 The Minimum Connect Offer

The first cycle will concentrate on three linked services.

Qualified intake and routing. The one-stop shop will receive European use cases,
support and expertise requests, adoption barriers, dependency needs and
contribution offers. It will qualify each request and route it to the most credible
existing support through national or local nodes, EDCOM partners, experts and
communities, procurement resources, challenges, EU-STF or other European
instruments. Decisions requiring DC-EDIC governance will go to the competent level.

Access will be online, complemented where useful by events, nodes and partner
facilities. The expertise hub will begin with active EDCOM and Strategy cases,
providing guidance or referrals where credible expertise is available. Its scope will
grow as expertise and capacity are confirmed.

The service will reuse European and national catalogue records, including the EU
Open Source Solutions Catalogue, through links, shared metadata and technical
federation where access and reuse conditions permit. It will add case qualification,
evidence and a practical next step, using structured metadata such as
publiccode.yml where useful.

For adoption cases, qualification will cover the user need, demonstrated maturity,
deployment conditions, available security and conformity evidence, support
providers, support and maintenance responsibilities and procurement routes.
Information will be dated and its limitations explicit.

Cooperation with the Open Internet Stack and public-sector Open Source
Programme Offices will connect users to existing discovery, assessment, support

3.3 Community Network And Local Nodes

The Community Network will connect actors to share cases, expertise and evidence.
Voluntary local nodes may coordinate national, regional, sectoral or thematic
participation where a capable organisation is willing to convene actors, qualify needs
or support participation.

Nodes will follow common participation and evidence principles; formal governance
rights remain those of the Statutes. They can make European cooperation accessible
across languages, administrative levels and levels of maturity.

EDCOM WP2 supports initial network coordination, shared methods and contact
points. National and local conveners connect users and contributors; the designated
DC-EDIC representative ensures alignment with common priorities and European
activities. Member State representatives and contact points help identify shared
needs, drawing first on contributions received. The Director and team compare
converging requirements and gaps with members and delivery partners to inform

3.4 State Of Digital Commons

The State of Digital Commons will provide a concise annual synthesis of identified
needs, relevant initiatives and lessons from Strategy activities. It will draw on existing
project reporting, Member State and observer knowledge, and selected research,
concentrating analysis on findings that change portfolio, contribution or governance
decisions.

Significant developments may prompt targeted updates between editions.
Recommendations will identify their sources, scope and intended audience. Work
will remain proportionate to delivery priorities, while EDCOM outputs retain their
agreed timetable.

4. Deploy: Making Adoption Work End To End

4.1 Purpose And Target Model

Deploy aims to give Member States and European organisations open-source
alternatives they can deploy at scale in key domains.

The DC-EDIC will define specific use cases in priority domains. With partners, it will
select mature software and components and mobilise the integration, operating and
maintenance capacity to deploy and sustain them.

Users and operators will validate solutions through concrete journeys covering
integration, identity and permissions, security and compliance, accessibility and user
experience, procurement and migration, organisational adoption, support and
maintenance. Priority will go to improvements in components, interfaces and
methods that other organisations can reuse.

Transparent criteria, open standards and interfaces, replaceable components and
shared tests will govern selection. Other actors can contribute and other solutions
can meet the same requirements. Organisations retain the ability to switch solutions.
Deployment findings will establish readiness, remaining barriers and choices to
revisit. The approach can be repeated in other domains.

4.2 First-Cycle Scope: Collaborative Workspace

The first proposed flagship will improve collaborative knowledge work across
organisations and countries. Five objectives guide the programme:

  1. Better tools for everyday work. Improve quality, accessibility, ease of use and
      continuity between tools, including useful AI assistance under user control.

 2. European interoperability that preserves trust. Enable collaboration across
      independently governed environments, with reliable identities, clear permissions
      and control over information.

 3. A diverse and innovative European market. Give communities and companies,
      including smaller providers, common foundations for differentiated products and
      services, while preserving users' ability to change tools or providers.

 4. Shared, high-quality building blocks maintained for the long term. Align
      technical priorities and contributions to development, integration, end-to-end
      testing, security and lifecycle care, reducing duplicated effort where joint work
      proves more effective.

 5. A contributing coalition around a common brand. Bring States, product teams,
      maintainers, providers and adopters together around a shared roadmap and
      concrete contributions. The programme brand will identify this effort and its
      results alongside participating product brands, under Section 8.5.

Members outside EDCOM can contribute alongside its funded work. Nationally
managed resources may support agreed common projects; participating teams
confirm their contributions and retain their product and deployment choices.

4.3 Choosing Interventions For Shared Value

The DC-EDIC and partners will use a consistent, proportionate approach to selecting
technologies, standards and delivery partners: start from a validated user need,
compare feasible options against the portfolio criteria and explain the choice through
existing programme records.

Selection will favour the strongest shared user benefit relative to full lifecycle costs
and risks, considering European added value and partner capabilities. Interventions
may support upstream improvements, shared components and interoperability, or
procurement, migration and adoption.

Shared foundations merit joint development where needs converge; distinct
products can improve usability, capability or resilience. Support will favour open,
modular assets that serve several solutions and remain replaceable under common
tests. Projects and maintainers will agree contributions, integration and maintenance
arrangements. Product owners retain responsibility for product-specific
development and fixes; shared dependency weaknesses go to Maintain.

Where shared needs justify joint effort, the DC-EDIC will seek cooperation with the
Open Internet Stack (OIS), relevant EDICs and other ecosystem actors to identify and
develop reusable open digital assets. Contributions will build on existing assets and
community work. Each will enter the existing portfolio with a receiving owner, clear
reuse conditions and validated resources.

4.4 Delivering Through 100-Day Challenges

First-cycle challenges will improve existing open-source components where
needed to complete qualified use cases. Priority goes to general-purpose
commons serving several solutions, such as BlockNote for collaborative editing or
Matrix for real-time communication.

Member States and partners may initiate independently funded 100-day challenges
within the agreed strategic framework through a simple notification procedure.
Formal DC-EDIC approval is only required where central resources are involved or
where the challenge is to become part of the official DC-EDIC portfolio.

Challenges involving central resources or seeking inclusion in the official DC-EDIC
portfolio follow the two-phase process below:

Qualification. Proposals from Member States and observers, EDCOM partners,
users, product teams, maintainers or Connect are assessed for need, European
added value and feasibility. The phase ends with an approved launch brief covering:

 � Result and approach: the intended result and user benefit, blocker to resolve in
     100 days, reusable intervention, feasible alternatives within programme scope
     and chosen form of sharing.

 � People and resources: participating users and environments, delivery lead,
     confirmed resources, credible receiving owner, responsibilities and contribution
     arrangements.

 � Success and continuity: measurable success criteria, evidence for the next
     decision, and handover and maintenance arrangements.

5. Maintain: Critical Dependencies And Stewardship

5.1 Purpose

Maintain helps make digital commons dependable infrastructure by identifying
critical projects and organising action to secure, repair and sustain them. It builds
European understanding of shared dependencies and strengthens stewardship
through proportionate action. Funding is one instrument of this broader function.

Stewardship keeps maintenance, security, support and continuity responsibilities
visible and credible over time. Software ownership and direct maintenance remain
with the relevant projects, communities, companies or operators.

Maintain will combine real-deployment evidence, maintainer knowledge, security
signals and public-interest priorities to identify dependencies. It will assess where
European coordination can reduce shared risk or improve adoption confidence,
choose proportionate support or continuity responses and review results.
Assessments may change deployment choices, trigger remediation or continuity
work, connect projects to support or identify targeted financing needs.

EU-STF is the first identified financing route for targeted maintenance. Other
remediation or continuity actions require an existing partner route or additional
funding.

The initial DC-EDIC contribution to the European Digital Public Infrastructure Steward
Organisation envisaged by the Commission will be preparatory design input. Under
the Director's responsibility, its scope, expected output and means will be agreed
with the Commission and interested Member States and communities, taking
account of existing stewards.

The first cycle will test whether dependency evidence leads to credible interventions
that improve resilience with a proportionate burden on maintainers. The link between
deployment needs, interventions and results must remain visible.

5.2 Critical Dependency Intelligence

Critical Dependency Intelligence will regularly assess dependencies important to the
DC-EDIC portfolio and Member States. Assessments will combine evidence such as
prevalence in real use cases, functional importance, vulnerability, governance
health, maintainer capacity, public interest and the feasibility of useful intervention.

The DC-EDIC remains accountable for the questions addressed, assessment-
method integrity and links to portfolio decisions. Member States and observers,
EDCOM partners, security actors and maintainers provide specialist assessments
under appropriate delivery arrangements.

Each assessment must state sources, uncertainty and limits, drawing on maintainer
knowledge through proportionate reporting and engagement. It may identify
candidate interventions; funding follows the applicable governance and resource
process.

For selected cases, the assessment, funded work package or deployment record will
specify responsibilities, maintenance and security arrangements, support routes,
continuity gaps and a review point. Maintainers and delivery partners will carry out
resulting actions.

7. Conditions For Trustworthy Adoption

7.1 Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity is a condition of adoption from discovery to handover. Every
deployment or maintenance intervention identifies the security and software-supply-
chain baseline, assurance methods, responsibility for vulnerabilities, means to
remedy them and expected continuity arrangements.

Evidence remains proportionate to the use case and legal context. Relevant
obligations and practices may include the Cyber Resilience Act, NIS2, GDPR, supply-
chain security, secure hosting, privacy and security by design, zero-trust principles
and independent assessment. Existing European schemes and ENISA work will be
reused where appropriate. Assurance will also consider cryptographic agility and
readiness for future cryptographic transitions where relevant.

Identifying vulnerabilities must lead to credible means of remedying them. Where
stronger requirements create work for communities or maintainers, the intervention
examines the capacity and resources they need to meet them.

A security concern can reshape or stop a pilot. The concern, evidence, responsible
actor and review process are recorded to inform that decision.

7.2 Artificial Intelligence

AI can reduce the cost of producing code, tests, documentation, translations and
support. It can help analyse vulnerabilities, understand large codebases and operate
complex systems. This changes open-source economics and practice, potentially
allowing smaller communities and public-interest projects to achieve results
previously requiring much larger teams.

Faster code production can also increase review and maintenance workloads.
Generated contributions may weaken provenance or community trust. AI-enabled
features can create dependencies on models, data, compute, licences and external
services, and change users' understanding of responsibility when systems propose
or perform actions.

A dedicated AI workstream will open where AI materially changes a portfolio activity,
user journey, software-production process, security posture, maintenance burden or
strategic dependency. It will state the expected value, human-control arrangements,
data and compute needs, licensing and provenance conditions, security implications
and community effects. Assessment of a material AI-enabled capability also covers
exit conditions and costs if a model, provider, service or compute source becomes
unavailable or unacceptable.

Collaborative Workspace AI assistance must demonstrate reliability, remain
attributable and reviewable, and keep consequential actions under human control. In
Maintain, AI may support code understanding, testing and vulnerability handling
while preserving maintainer judgement and manageable community review
workloads.

The Future Domains Roadmap can assess AI-enabled open-source capabilities as a
possible domain. A general AI programme requires a later portfolio and governance
decision.

7.3 Interoperability, Portability And Exit

European reuse depends on interoperability. Assessments will address legal,
organisational, semantic and technical interoperability, drawing proportionately on
the European Interoperability Framework and applying Interoperable Europe Act
requirements where relevant. Open source supports transparency, reuse and
adaptation; technological neutrality and effective switching also require open
standards, compatible interfaces and evidence from deployment and substitution
tests. The Strategy favours open standards and formats, explicit interfaces and
documented conformance methods. Deployment assessments will establish whether
data, permissions and relevant workflows retain acceptable integrity and usability
across components and organisations. Cross-organisational or cross-country proofs
will check consistent meanings for shared information and access rights, with
explicit mappings where practices or languages differ.

Procurement arrangements and reference configurations will make migration
assumptions visible, preserve auditability and favour practically replaceable
dependencies. Portability includes the ability to leave a solution: a substitution test is
often more informative than a general claim of modularity.

Transition evidence will separate the cost of leaving the incumbent and reaching a
neutral, usable interface from the cost of adopting the target solution. This keeps
total transition effort visible without attributing accumulated lock-in to the alternative.

The DC-EDIC may publish profiles, requirements and test evidence during the first
cycle. A future certification or label role requires separate legal, governance,
technical, market-neutrality and maintenance analysis and an appropriate decision.

Where deployment reveals a standards gap, the DC-EDIC will raise it through the
relevant European or international standardisation and interoperability channel and
support the competent process.

7.4 User Experience And Organisational Adoption

Usability determines whether people can adopt an interoperable, secure capability.
Each proof includes representative users and considers accessibility, design,
multilingual use, onboarding, support and the complete journey's coherence.

Organisational adoption receives the same rigour as code. Management support,
political confidence, procurement readiness, training, migration planning and
operating responsibility can determine success. As it moves to regular use, each
participating organisation will identify its adoption lead and the resources needed to
support users. Evidence will distinguish a functioning prototype from a capability the
organisation can operate.

7.5 European Ecosystem And Economic Capacity

Digital commons rely on viable organisations as well as code. Communities and
foundations provide governance and knowledge; maintainers sustain the technical
base. Open-source companies, integrators, hosters and support providers turn
components into dependable services. Public administrations and other anchor
users bring demand, evidence and continuity.

Shared software foundations allow several companies to offer integration, hosting,
training and support services. The aim is to widen buyers' effective choice and
enable European providers, particularly SMEs, to develop their business across
countries using common resources.

The first cycle strengthens their combined delivery capacity around concrete use
cases. Transparent requirements and procurement methods can help European
SMEs and integrators meet demand. Reference implementations can reveal support
or integration gaps; maintenance interventions can improve trusted maintainers'
working conditions; reusable evidence can lower entry costs in another national
market.

The DC-EDIC will use open contribution routes, objective criteria and conflict-of-
interest safeguards. Ecosystem growth will be demonstrated by European actors'
ability to deliver, support and sustain capabilities users need.

9.2 Assessing Real-World Impact

Each operating product will be assessed on improvements for beneficiaries
beyond its deliverables. At the start, its lead will define the intended change with
beneficiaries. The existing charter will record a baseline, review date and a few
simple indicators, with quantified objectives where meaningful, refined as initial
measurements become available. Monitoring will be proportionate to resources and
stakes.

Indicators may track a procurement route actually used, support capacity available in
another country, the effort needed to repeat a deployment, or maintenance
resources confirmed beyond initial funding.

The team and partners will test improvements with users in short cycles and adjust
priorities from feedback. Usage data and feedback from operators, maintainers and
receiving owners will show what improves, for whom, what remains difficult and how
far the action explains observed changes. Findings will be dated, sourced and
explicit about limitations and disappointing results.

Continuation and expansion depend on benefits relative to costs and the DC-

9.3 Review And Learning

Review frequency will follow decision needs. Reviews consider results and evidence,
user or decision-maker responses, material risks and resources, then ask:

 � Are Connect, Deploy and Maintain achieving the outcomes in Section 9.1 and
     reinforcing one another?

 � Are resources concentrated on the highest shared value?
 � What did the DC-EDIC make possible, faster, more reliable or reusable across

     countries, and was it worth the coordination effort?
 � Which product stops, adjusts, continues, transitions or scales?

Stopping ends an intervention with insufficient value or evidence; the underlying
strategic need returns for a fresh decision on how the portfolio should address it.
Adjusting means fixing, improving, narrowing or redesigning the intervention before
its next review.

Annually, the Director will submit an implementation report to the Assembly covering
the portfolio, material evidence, resources, unresolved risks and proposed
amendments. After Assembly approval, it will be transmitted to the Commission
under Article 24. The annual activity report is also made public under Article 19.

9.4 Risks

The main strategic risks are:

Portfolio dispersion. Eight products may be read as eight simultaneous
programmes. Proportionate scopes, sequencing and activation conditions keep
effort focused.

Activity disconnected from adoption. Networks, challenges or pilots may generate
activity but little improvement in user journeys or decisions. Every product will be
judged against its expected result.

Fragmentation. National configurations, projects or standards may diverge. Shared
use cases, interfaces, tests and reusable evidence provide common reference
points.

Winner-picking or market capture. Reference implementations may be mistaken for
endorsed products. Modular requirements, transparent criteria, substitution tests and
conflict-of-interest safeguards preserve neutrality.

Prospective capacity. Delivery may rely on unconfirmed partners, funding or staff.
Activation requires named ownership, a credible delivery route and proportionate
resources.

Delegated delivery losing strategic control. A partner could become the de facto
owner of European priorities. The DC-EDIC defines the mandate, safeguards,
evidence requirements and review points, preserving accountability while drawing
efficiently on partners' operational expertise.

Maintenance after pilot delivery. Outputs may lack a receiving owner or continuity
path. Challenges and pilots require handover and maintenance conditions before
launch.

Administrative burden. Governance and reporting may slow intended beneficiaries.
Evidence and accountability requirements will be designed with users and
maintainers and reviewed for proportionality.

Unfunded security remediation. Stronger assurance may reveal issues lacking a
resourced response. Security review must identify the remedy and responsible actor.

Roadmap accumulation. Future themes may become implicit commitments. Active
work requires accountable preparation, confirmed capacity and an explicit launch,
prepare, defer or stop decision for every theme.

Loss of trust. Overclaiming country support, funding, product readiness or European
consensus would weaken the Strategy. Source status and decision authority remain
visible.

Existing project and portfolio reporting will record risk likelihood and impact, the
accountable owner, mitigation actions, action deadlines and the next review date.
The Director and designated DC-EDIC representatives will establish this view during
initial implementation, using existing programme records, and update it at product
reviews, escalating material risks through the reporting and decision rights in
Sections 8.1, 8.7 and 9.3.

10. Implementation Commitment

This Strategy commits the DC-EDIC to turning European cooperation into capabilities
that people and organisations can use.

Seven operating products implement Connect, Deploy and Maintain. Their results will
test whether European coordination makes adoption repeatable and critical
commons resilient, while preserving solution diversity and choice. The eighth result,
the Future Domains Roadmap, identifies later options for explicit decisions. Evidence
will inform decisions on what to repeat, fund, sustain or stop.

Different national approaches and open participation by communities and market
actors must produce shared results. Cooperation needs action on agreed priorities
and clear delivery and maintenance responsibilities. Through this work, Europe will
build the capacity to develop, deploy and maintain digital commons collectively and
sustainably.

Annex C. Common Portfolio Review Criteria

Product charters remain the detailed management reference for users, scope,
delivery arrangements and evidence. Six common criteria guide decisions on all
eight expected results:

  1. Strategic relevance: a clear shared need, fit with the statutory mandate and
      European added value.

 2. Responsibility: both a named DC-EDIC point of accountability and a credible
      delivery lead or arrangement are confirmed.

 3. Impact and evidence: observed improvements for beneficiaries, product and
      DC-EDIC contributions, and reliable, traceable sources support assessment at
      the relevant stage of work.

 4. Resources: scope matches available DC-EDIC, EDCOM, Member State and
      partner capacity.

 5. Trust and continuity: proportionate treatment of security, neutrality, conflicts of
      interest, support and maintenance.

 6. Next decision: evidence supports an explicit choice: stop, adjust, continue,
      transition or scale for operating results; launch, prepare, defer or stop for
      roadmap themes.

For the EU-STF Pilot, these criteria apply at three first-cycle decision points: design
review, coordinated cohort selection and end-2027 review, under the responsibilities
in Section 5.3. Technical assessment and routine delivery follow confirmed delivery
arrangements unless a material exception requires DC-EDIC action.
